The 2026 S.-T. Yau Contest
My experience with the 2026 Algebra and Number Theory exam.

The S.-T. Yau College Student Mathematics Contest is an undergraduate competition founded by Shing-Tung Yau in 2010. I took the Algebra and Number Theory exam. The format was five problems in two hours, modeled closely after graduate entrance exams. I ended up getting 39th place, which is an honorable mention, and a result I'm very happy with.

A Problem From 2025

To give an idea of what the exam is like, here is Problem 5 from the 2025 Algebra and Number Theory paper.

Let $f(X)=X^5-X+1\in\mathbb Q[X]$, let $E$ be its splitting field over $\mathbb Q$, and set $D=\operatorname{Disc}(f)$. Recall that $\operatorname{Disc}(X^5+aX+b)=4^4a^5+5^5b^4$.

  1. Show that $f$ is irreducible.
  2. Determine all finite primes ramified in $E$ and compute the orders of their inertia subgroups.
  3. Show that $E/\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ is unramified at every finite prime and ramified at the Archimedean primes.
  4. Show that $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)$ is generated by its inertia subgroups.
  5. Show that $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong S_5$ and $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q(\sqrt D))\cong A_5$.

Here is an extremely abbreviated solution. The reduction of $f$ modulo $3$ is irreducible, while $D=5^5-4^4=2869=19\cdot151$ is square-free. Thus the only ramified primes are $19$ and $151$, and their inertia groups have order two and act as transpositions. The group generated by all finite inertia groups is normal, and its fixed field is unramified over $\mathbb Q$ at every finite prime; Minkowski therefore says that the fixed field is $\mathbb Q$. Consequently the Galois group is a transitive subgroup of $S_5$ generated by transpositions, hence is $S_5$. The square root of the discriminant transforms by the sign character, giving the $A_5$ claim and finite unramifiedness over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$. Finally, $f$ has only one real root, so $E$ is totally imaginary whereas $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ is real; its Archimedean primes ramify.

The 2026 Exam

Here is the full 2026 Algebra and Number Theory exam.

My favorite problem was Problem 3. Let $K$ be a number field and $\mathcal O_{\overline K}$ the ring of all algebraic integers in an algebraic closure. The problem asks you to prove that $a,b\in\mathcal O_K$ are coprime if and only if there is some $u\in\mathcal O_{\overline K}$ for which $au+b$ is a unit.

Here is a solution sketch. Suppose $(a,b)=1$; the trivial cases aside, choose $n$ such that $(-b)^n\equiv1\pmod a$, which is possible because $(\mathcal O_K/(a))^\times$ is finite. Since the degree-$(n-1)$ monomials in $a$ and $-b$ generate $(a,b)^{n-1}=(1)$, we can choose $c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1}\in\mathcal O_K$ so that

$$ (-b)^n+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j a^{n-j}(-b)^j=1. $$

Set $P(T)=T^n+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_jT^j$ and take any root $u$ of $P$. Then $u$ is integral, while $a^nP((Y-b)/a)$ is a monic integral polynomial with constant term $1$ having $au+b$ as a root, so $au+b$ is a unit. Conversely, if a prime ideal contained both $a$ and $b$, any prime above it in a finite extension containing $u$ and $(au+b)^{-1}$ would contain the unit $au+b$, a contradiction.

I liked this problem because even if you know all the machinery, it is still quite hard to come up with this solution. It was a very rewarding experience to solve this one.

The Experience

After solving Problems 3 and 4, there was barely any time left. This was despite the fact that neither took extremely long to think through, especially Problem 4, which was a mechanical application of Dedekind's criterion. Writing the solutions consumed almost the entire exam. I suspect my writeups were much more verbose then they needed to be, I will try to correct this next year.

I searched for partials in many of the other problems, though without having a score breakdown, I'm not sure how successful I was. I was particularly sad about Problem 5: I had not read far enough into Neukirch to know the relevant local-field material, but only barely, I had stopped just before the useful part. In the end, I ran out of time trying to get a Hermite normal form solution working for problem 1.

I also made the bad decision of not attempting Problem 2 because its statement looked so long and I was in a time crunch. It turned out to be by far the freest problem on the exam, and solving it would have gotten me to finals.

Still, I am hopeful that I can make finals next year. Congratulations to my friend Jack for making it this time! Amusingly, we solved almost disjoint sets of problems, so together we would have made one excellent contestant. Unfortunately, the individual contest remains stubbornly individual.

My Opinion

Overall, the exam is very fun and rewarding. It is fun to test one's mettle against what is essentially a souped up graduate qualifying exam. It's also rare that one gets to use advanced machinery in a competition format.

The exam itself is very different from the other math competitions I've done. With exceptions, it is sometimes a knowledge check, and a lot of questions can be boiled down to:

Do you know this machinery, and how fast can you write it up?

Due to the insane time constraints, it's mandatory to have seen a good amount of the problems (many of which are standard textbook exercises) almost verbatim. Problem 4 is a good example, I got lucky to have studied Dedekind's criterion, otherwise it would have taken way longer. It is therefore very important how well the exam overlaps with your knowledge, and how fast of a writer you are (this might be the reason the winners are very variable each year, without many repeat successes). This is not a bad thing though, knowing machinery is extremely important, and it's very rewarding to read a problem and instantly say "I know how to do this, I read about it in X textbook".

My only complaint is the distribution of problems. Despite allegedly being about algebra and number theory, this year's exam was 5/5 problems on number theory. There doesn't seem to be any standard for what the format should be. I recall "strategically" sacrificing Riemannian geometry in my preparations for the Topology and Geometry Exam. I still remember the sheer hopelessness I felt in my soul upon being greeted by 5/6 problems requiring a strong base in Riemannian geometry to solve. I'm certainly not going to make this mistake again next year.
