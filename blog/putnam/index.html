Math Competitions
My preparation for and feelings towards the Putnam exam, and math competitions in general.

I tentatively believe that I have managed to give a very good performance in the Putnam exam. Of course, I still need to wait for scores to come out, but after looking at AOPS solutions and comparing to mine I'm confident that I did extremely well.

Mindset

Math competitions have probably been my biggest hobby since high school (about seven years running). Broadly, I would conjecture that math competitions test the following things in decreasing order of importance:

  1. Your problem solving abilities and intuition, especially as it pertains to the topics covered by the exam (elem nmbr theory, combo, etc)
  2. Your ability to handle extreme pressure
  3. Your luck, how well the problems are suited to your skills
  4. Your speed

Due to studying so much over a long period of time, and my mock test performances, I would claim that I am in decent standing in terms of (1), at least in the subjects that I focus on. However (2) is a big issue for me. When you are studying hours a day for a competition that shows up only once a year, and you've historically had disappointing performances, that kind of pressure can annihilate your sleep the night before and make you unable to even read the problem statements correctly.

In terms of getting over anxiety on math competitions, a good way might be to fully dissociate yourself from the result of the competition. This is of course easier said than done, but I would say I have achieved this on the most recent contest. Another important factor was the testing room. UChicago as a whole does not care much about math competitions. We've previously been packed into a tiny lecture hall, with those crappy desks with a fold out contraption that can't even fit a single piece of paper (If you're in college, I know you know what I'm talking about). I was bumping elbows with the people next to me since every single seat was filled. If you ask me, this is extremely disrespectful to hundreds or thousands of hours students put into this exam. We only ask to be accommodated a single day out of the entire year.

After a lot of emailing and pushing towards the administration, we managed to get a whopping two rooms this year. Unfortunately, we still had the fold out contraptions instead of a real desk, however, there at least were two feet of space between me and the next student and a place to put my pens and scratch paper other than the floor.

I will say that on this most recent competition, I felt calm, focused, and relaxed. I even managed to sleep the night before the exam. This definitely boosted my performance. Even besides these factors, the test also felt perfectly suited for my skillset (factor (3)). The analysis problems (my worst subject) were all early in the exam, and the "deciding problems" in the 3-5 range were quite algebraic. My favorite categories in the MIT sheets are linear algebra, algebra, and congruences, so you might guess that this past exam would suit me.

Preparations

In order to prepare for the 2024 competition, I did nearly all the exam problems from the past 15ish years. The downside to this is that going into 2025 I had "run out" of problems to do since the earlier years are not very representative of the modern exam. Therefore, I needed a new source of problems to do.

I figured that the MIT students are always dominating the Putnam, and a quick google search pulls up many claims that Yufei Zhao has perfected the art of training Putnam competitors (a claim I wholeheartedly believe). Therefore, I started doing the problem sheets from their Putnam seminar. I must deeply thank Professor Zhao for putting these problems online.

Over a very long period of time I managed to finish a large amount of the problems. In terms of how I did these problems, this was my procedure:

  1. Select a category based on my mood and vibes.
  2. Try to solve a problem; if I can't I'll come back to it later or collaborate with someone else (usually my friend Jack).
  3. If I do solve the problem, I'll write up a solution sketch to make sure I'm not fooling myself.
  4. After solving, I'll try to find other solutions online or with AI. Then I'll compare and possibly change my solution to be more clean and elegant. I am generally extremely concerned about having the "best" solution to every problem, and I feel great regret in seeing a better solution even if I did manage to solve a problem. There is also some great satisfaction in really digging into the core reason why something is true and exposing it fully in the most direct and efficient way.
  5. Finally, I will sometimes explore uncovered connections to higher math topics. The problems in the MIT sheets are very nice in the sense that there are many such connections, especially in categories such as linear algebra. Finding and exploring them allows one to get more out of each problem.

If you are interested, here is a sample of my solution (sketches) that I decided to TeX up on the linear algebra category. If you want to discuss any problems on the MIT sheets feel free to email me, of course, there is a chance I won't have done it, in particular if it's on the more analytic side or very bashy.

I must say doing these problems helped me tremendously, and I would recommend them to everyone who is taking the Putnam competition seriously. In all honestly, there is no way to get good at math competitions except doing a ton of problems. Of course, there are a lot of problems on the sheets, some of which are P5-P6 level, so it goes without saying solving any significant amount them is a massive endeavor. If you don't have much time, doing all the hearted problems is a good middle ground. They tend to be the type of problems that really teach you something when you solve it.
