The Prestige of Philosophy
A Case Against Putting Philosophy on a Pedestal

It seems almost tradition for personal websites to contain at least one post on philosophy. Moreover, I often see posts of the form "A Case for Philosophy". I suppose this post might be me being a contrarian.

I generally don't like philosophy. And by philosophy, throughout the post I am generally referring to the very academic style philosophy found in my core classes. Doing philosophy (whatever that means) isn't really something that I enjoy. To be clear, I don't think there's anything wrong with doing philosophy or not doing philosophy. As far as I'm concerned, it can be both an interesting hobby and field of study, and those who do it should continue if it brings them joy and beauty.

On the other hand, it seems common for people to believe that doing philosophy allows one to be "enlightened". Or in a more general sense, that one needs to study philosophy in order to be smart, or wise, or to attain some other vague positive trait. This is especially true at UChicago, where we are forced to take ridiculously philosophical core classes, with no more practical alternatives almost by design. There is also a belief that every smart or intellectual person must have sophisticated philosophical beliefs and understanding. I suppose this post is me lashing out against this idea, and justifying why the philosophers should leave me alone.

Essentially, philosophy has a unique aura. For example, people seem to believe that in order to be "educated" in a very broad sense, you need to understand philosophy, whereas the fields of biology or physics are not given the same treatment. My main claim is a rejection of this, I believe that philosophy should not be put on a special pedestal above other subjects. In the same way that the average mathematician is not expected to understand much biology, there is no particular reason to expect that someone in a different field ought to understand much philosophy. I will try to deconstruct the reasons people give for why philosophy is somehow special and necessary.

Does Philosophy Make You Happier?

This is a funny question, because a lot of philosophically oriented people I ask this give me a resounding no. There is a stereotype of the "miserable philosopher". Nonetheless, claim it improves your well-being by allowing you to understand your purpose, or self actualize, or something like that. Let's try to answer the question with the scientific method.

Applied philosophy

Claim: Applied philosophy shows some evidence of improving happiness-related outcomes, but the evidence is mixed and depends a lot on the kind of philosophy. Unfortunately literature is very limited.

  • LeBon et al. (2025) — This paper develops a scale for philosophical Stoicism as a way of life rather than "stoicism" in the ordinary repression sense. Higher scores on that scale were associated with higher life satisfaction, flourishing, and more positive affect balance. So this is evidence that practiced Stoicism can be linked to being happier.
  • Hofmann et al. (2014) — This meta-analysis found that CBT for anxiety disorders was moderately effective at improving quality of life, especially in the physical and psychological domains. That is stronger empirical evidence than most of the philosophy papers that structured cognitive intervention can improve happiness-adjacent outcomes.
  • Maj et al. (2023) — This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that internet-delivered CBT for depression and anxiety had small to moderate positive effects on quality of life. So if the question is whether reflective cognitive practice can improve well-being, CBT has a better empirical case than most philosophical interventions.

Abstract philosophy

Claim: There is little direct empirical evidence that studying abstract philosophy makes people happier.

  • Baetens et al. (2022) — In a large study of university students' subjective well-being during COVID, students from the Literature and Philosophy faculty reported the lowest subjective well-being, though the paper also says the faculty differences were small.
  • Are Philosophers Happy? — A blog post analyzing the International Wellbeing Study. When compared directly to their non-philosopher peers, 96 philosophers who were encouraged to take it were found to be slightly less happy across three study groups.
  • As a whole, there aren't many direct empirical papers addressing the question.

Overall conclusion

It seems that the type of philosophy you do matters. More prescriptive philosophies like Stoicism sometimes seem to help, while more abstract ones do not have much direct evidence behind them. I personally should probably do some more prescriptive philosophy. However when people ask this question, they usually mean abstract philosophy. And moreover that is the kind of philosophy that is the subject of this post. Unfortunately, this isn't really a question I can find much literature on, especially with regards to abstract philosophy. Maybe people don't really care to actually find out the answer to this question.

Does Philosophy Make You Rational?

Almost everyone would probably hold this view. It seems quite obvious on a philosophical level, but is it actually true? Probably somewhat.

Measurable reasoning skills

Claim: Philosophy does seem to improve certain reasoning-related metrics.

  • Prinzing (2024) — Review of the literature: philosophers and philosophy students tend to score better on logical reasoning, reflection, and open-mindedness measures.
  • Prinzing (2025) — Stronger evidence that even after accounting for baseline differences, philosophy majors still outperform other majors on verbal and logical reasoning tests and on some “habits of mind” measures.
  • ETS GRE data — Philosophy students do extremely well on standardized tests, ranking at or near the top in verbal reasoning and analytical writing, and doing very well overall.

So I will concede the obvious point: philosophy really does seem to help with the kinds of things that are easy to measure, like argument analysis, verbal precision, and formal reasoning.

Biases

Claim: This does not mean philosophers are broadly more rational, or less vulnerable to ordinary cognitive biases.

  • Schwitzgebel & Cushman (2015) — Professional philosophers showed substantial framing effects and order effects on moral dilemmas, and were no less susceptible than other academics.
  • Fischer, Engelhardt, & Herbelot (2022) — Philosophers were better at deploying concepts, but were susceptible to linguistic salience bias to a similar extent as comparison subjects.

It seems like philosophy helps with certain intellectual skills, but philosophers are still normal humans. They are equally susceptible to framing, wording, salience, and the usual assortment of psychological nonsense.

Overall conclusion

Philosophy probably makes you better at explicit reasoning tasks. It probably does not make you particularly more free from bias, or hyper-rational in any sense. That is weaker than the grand claim that philosophy makes you rational in life as a whole. In fact, I would claim that you can achieve the same effect by really studying anything that is very "logical" in college, like physics or math, which also increase your LSAT or GRE scores. Philosophy really has no monopoly on abstract reasoning.

Does Philosophy Improve Your Character?

There is a whole lot of philosophy revolving around what people ought to do in all sorts of situations in order to be good people. I suppose that is called ethics. But the question is, does studying ethics actually make you a better person?

Overall

Claim: There is little evidence that studying ethics or doing moral philosophy makes people behave better in ordinary life.

  • Schwitzgebel & Rust (2014) — This is probably the central paper on the topic. They compared ethics professors, non-ethicist philosophers, and other professors across a range of measures such as charitable giving, voting, vegetarianism, staying in touch with one's mother, and responsiveness to student emails. The overall result was not flattering: ethicists did not behave better on average.
  • Schwitzgebel (2009) — This paper examined rates at which ethics books were missing from major academic libraries, compared to similar non-ethics philosophy books. Contemporary ethics books were more likely to be missing, and classic ethics books were about twice as likely to be missing. So even on a very crude behavioral measure, people especially interested in ethics do not seem morally improved.

So the obvious thought, that people who spend their lives talking about morality must surely be very moral, seems mostly false. While moral philosophers may become better at describing moral considerations, that is not the same thing as being moral.

Relativist philosophy

Claim: Philosophies which make morality seem subjective or relative may actually make people behave worse.

  • Rai & Holyoak (2013) — Across two experiments, exposure to moral relativist arguments made participants more likely to cheat or behave less ethically, while more absolutist framings reduced this effect. That is about as direct a result as one could ask for.
  • Young & Durwin (2013) — Priming participants toward moral realism increased charitable giving. The basic idea is simple: if morality feels objective, then moral demands feel more real, and people act accordingly.
  • Barnett, Bass, & Brown (1996) — In a study of cheating-related judgments, individuals with a more non-relativistic ethical outlook were more likely to judge reporting cheating as ethical, and more likely to say they would report it.

This seems right to me on an intuitive level too. If you think that morality is ultimately just perspective, convention, language game, culture, or taste, then it becomes much easier to excuse bad behavior. You may still be able to speak elegantly about injustice. But when the moment comes to actually do the right thing at some personal cost, "it's all relative" is a very convenient philosophy. It just doesn't do a good job of fighting human nature, which is to try and rationalize selfishness as much as your moral framework will allow.

Overall Conclusion

Philosophy does not seem to improve character in any empirical sense. Studying ethics does not appear to make people noticeably more ethical. And some forms of philosophy, especially relativist ones, may do the opposite by making wrongdoing feel negotiable. Therefore there is no justification for forcing people to study philosophy so that they may be better citizens or more moral people.

I suppose my (relatively subjective) opinion is as follows. At best, philosophy can give you a vocabulary for talking about virtue. At worst, it gives clever people more advanced tools to help them rationalize bad stuff they already wanted to do, or feel less guilty for the stuff they already did. That doesn't really seem worthwhile if your goal is becoming a better person.

Philosophizing Against Philosophy

In the end, the grand justification for philosophy just does not seem to hold up. If the claim is that philosophy makes you happier, the evidence is weak at best for the kind of abstract philosophy people usually mean. If the claim is that it makes you more rational, that is certainly true in a specific sense, but philosophy doesn't have some unique pedestal over other disciplines on this front. And if the claim is that it improves character, the evidence is even less flattering: people who study ethics do not seem to behave better, and some philosophical views seem to encourage bad behavior. So the belief I started with still seems right to me. Philosophy is certainly enjoyable and beautiful for some, but for the average person there is no good reason to treat it as a special path to enlightenment, wisdom, or virtue. And if that is all philosophy can claim, then the philosophers really should leave the rest of us alone.

At this point, I will anticipate some expected responses. This part will probably be a bit more philosophical, and will address the arguments that can't really be tested empirically (which is most of them), so get ready to read a lot of opinions.

1. Everything is actually philosophy

Many might claim that I am doing philosophy by writing this post (probably true), or when I'm doing math (probably false). I guess that depends on how you define philosophy (getting to the next point). However in this blog post I am talking about a very specific, academic, humanities style section of philosophy. The sort of stuff you might find in a book with the word philosophy on the cover. I'm not really concerned with someone else's all-encompassing definition of philosophy.

Nonetheless, If you believe effectively everything is philosophy, aren't I already doing enough of it? In this paradigm, there is no reason to force me to study Socrates in my classes because I'm already doing my own philosophy in my own way. Essentially, if your train of thought is:

BLANK is actually philosophy → philosophy is important and should be studied → everyone should read Socrates

doesn't the generality of the premise invalidate the specificity of the conclusion?

2. Philosophy helps you understand the world

The claim here is that studying philosophy allows one to understand the world in some kind of unique and special way. Therefore since understanding the world is good, and perhaps one of the goals of higher education, everyone should read Socrates.

This is a very vague claim. What is understanding the world? If you want to understand the laws the world follows, study physics. If you want to understand the living things around you, study biology, if you want to understand people or yourself, study psychology, etc etc. So what’s left for philosophy? I’m told my opinion on this matter is that of Wittgenstein. That is, philosophy is the study of getting confused by the vagueness inherent to language. Or more eloquently, an activity of language analysis designed to dissolve pseudo-problems. This is perfectly exemplified by the current topic. The question “does philosophy help you understand the world” entirely reduces to what you mean by understanding the world, and what you mean by philosophy.

The ship of Theseus is another example. The ship is what it is, a particular collection of matter. What you call it afterwards isn’t really interesting. After all, words are just things we use to communicate. Now, many will say not all philosophy is like that. This depends on how you define philosophy (touché). But broadly, I would disagree for what most people refer to as philosophy, if you dig deep enough, it’s almost always just words. Therefore the only way to make progress in philosophy and resolve disagreements is to figure how to actually define the question you are asking rigorously, at which point you might as well be doing math.

This is a philosophical claim, so I can’t really prove I’m right, and I’m not motivated to try. However if you want an argument, maybe read Wittgenstein. He probably explains what I believe in a much more effective, elegant, and unbiased way. I suppose the irony of me quoting a famous philosopher is quite amusing.

In terms of an example, consider Alan Turing (who studied math) making a mathematical framework for computability, at which point the field of philosophy decided to take credit since this "resolved" vague questions about what problems we can solve. However it is worth noting this only happened after Hilbert (another mathematician) figured out how to ask the question in a productive, rigorous way (the Entscheidungsproblem). One can debate whether "philosophy" really helped that much, however I'm more inclined to believe that it was mainly mathematicians doing mathematics, trying to answer a question about math (even if Hilbert was also a philosopher).

Regardless, even if you don't necessarily subscribe to my extremist beliefs, there is certainly no justification that the understanding of the world you get from reading Socrates is inherently better than what you get from studying physics, or biology, or math.

3. Science comes from philosophy

This claim comes in many forms. But generally the gist is philosophy taking credit for some other thing that people universally agree is very useful and helpful, and therefore I need to read Socrates. This is quite similar to the first point, but subtly different.

The argument usually goes something like this: physics used to be called "natural philosophy," and figures like Descartes and Leibniz were both mathematicians and philosophers. Therefore, modern science and math are offshoots of philosophy, and moreover rely on philosophical assumptions, so those who practice these disciplines owe philosophy respect and time. This is essentially a bait-and-switch. It assumes that the "philosophy" done by Newton is the exact same discipline being taught in a modern humanities seminar today.

It is historically true that philosophy used to be a massive, catch-all term for "thinking deeply about things." But when a branch of philosophy actually develops a rigorous, objective methodology. It graduates. It becomes physics, or biology, or mathematics.

What is left behind in the philosophy department? The questions that cannot be answered with rigor or data. The linguistic ambiguities, the unprovable premises, and the pseudo-problems. If a problem can actually be solved, it gets solved in a STEM department. Otherwise, people turn it into a language game and kick it around. Occasionally, a related scientific advancement exposes a more testable question we ought to be asking.

If you're only interested in these testable questions, it is strictly not mandatory to participate in the "kicking it around". After all, many great scientific advancements are made by people who don't like doing philosophy. And when a philosopher does make a scientific or mathematical discovery, they are always also a scientist or a mathematician. Therefore, there is no reason to think being a philosopher has a causal relationship on being a good scientist or mathematician, at least in the modern age where the overlap has shrunk. Therefore someone interested in science should not be forced to read Socrates for the sake of being a good scientist.

As an example, we universally recognize that chemistry evolved from alchemy, and astronomy evolved from astrology. Yet, we don't force modern chemists to study the philosopher's stone to understand molecular bonding. Similarly, a modern physicist or mathematician does not need to read Plato to understand thermodynamics or commutative algebra. Recognizing the historical roots of science is fun for those who are inclined, but it doesn't justify why someone needs to spend their time reading ancient metaphysics instead of doing what they actually enjoy. And moreover, I would be extremely surprised if someone found evidence that studying philosophy somehow makes one better at math or science. I am inclined to believe that this not true, in the same way that studying alchemy probably doesn't make you better at organic chemistry.
